Ion–Surface Interaction

The software computes what happens when ions strike a solid surface: reflection and backscattering of the projectiles, sputtering of the target, and the charge state of the returning and the sputtered particles. The collision kinematics are computed with the binary collision code RustBCA; charge exchange at the surface is added on top by a dedicated three-channel model. The target range is low-energy ion–surface interaction from about 1 eV up into the keV range.

What is this software for?

In electric space propulsion and in surface analysis, ions of low and medium energy strike materials. This interaction determines the quantities of practical importance: how many ions are backscattered, and with what energy and angular distribution? How strongly is the material eroded? And in which charge state do the particles leave the surface – a question that is crucial for any ion-optical or mass-spectrometric diagnostic, because only charged particles are detected there.

The program combines both aspects in a single interface. The collision kinematics provide the energy and exit angle of every returning or sputtered particle; from these follows the normal velocity that governs charge exchange with the surface. Reflection, sputtering and charge state are thus determined consistently from a single simulation run and can be assessed directly against each other.

Physical models and features

The functionality is modular. Depending on the question at hand, projectile, target, surface shape, interaction potential and charge model can be combined freely.

  • Projectiles – noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe) and the reactive elements H, N, O, plus molecular ions (O₂⁺, N₂⁺, NO⁺, H₂⁺) via the dissociation approximation. Any further elements can be defined freely by atomic number and mass.
  • Target materials – 18 element presets with literature values for surface binding energy and number density, compounds and alloys (TiO₂, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, TiN, brass, stainless steel) and a builder for custom compounds with up to four components and free stoichiometry.
  • Surface geometry – semi-infinite smooth surface, sphere for nanoparticles, layer on substrate, rough surface with adjustable amplitude and wavelength in two or three dimensions, and import of custom triangle meshes in OBJ format.
  • Interaction potentials – screened Coulomb potentials (KR_C, ZBL, Molière, TRIDYN, Lenz–Jensen) for the standard case, and attractive potentials (Morse, Lennard-Jones, WW, KRC-Morse, 4-8) with freely adjustable parameters for chemically bound and very light species.
  • Numerical methods – selectable scattering integrals (Mendenhall–Weller, MAGIC, Gaussian quadrature), root finders (Newton, CPR, polynomial), models for electronic stopping and for the mean free path. When the potential is changed, the program automatically sets a valid combination.
  • Surface binding model – planar or isotropic surface barrier and selectable assignment of the binding energy to projectile, target or the average of both. This makes it possible to study how the surface potential affects the exit energy of reflected particles and, consequently, their charge state.
  • Charge exchange as a three-channel model – Auger neutralisation after Hagstrum for the positive channel, resonant charge transfer after Los and Geerlings for the negative channel, the neutral fraction as the remainder. The work function of the target and the electron affinity of the projectile enter explicitly; the characteristic velocities can be calibrated against measurements.
  • Sputtering – yield, energy and angular distribution of the sputtered atoms, resolved by element for compounds with partial yields, so that preferential sputtering becomes visible.
  • Secondary ions and SIMS spectrum – ionisation probability of the sputtered atoms from the ionisation potential, electron affinity and work function; from this a mass spectrum versus mass-to-charge ratio with natural isotope distribution, either positive or negative.
  • Particle trajectories – display of the ion and recoil trajectories in the target as a two-dimensional section and as a rotatable three-dimensional view, coloured by energy or by element.
  • Parameter studies – automated series over energy, angle of incidence or material selection. All key figures are computed for each grid point; the display fills progressively and can be paused at any time.
  • Analysis and export – graphics export with selectable background, label language, resolution and colour palette, and export of all raw data as CSV with a self-describing metadata header in line with the FAIR principles.

Reflection, sputtering and charge state

A single simulation run yields several analyses, which the program provides in separate tabs.

  • Energy–angle correlation – a two-dimensional histogram of the reflected particles shows the relationship between exit energy and exit angle that is lost in separate one-dimensional distributions. It can optionally be displayed as a rotatable three-dimensional landscape.
  • Energy spectrum of the sputtered atoms – the spectrum shows the characteristic Thompson maximum at about half the surface binding energy. The effective surface binding energy is recovered from the superimposed model curve.
  • Secondary ion mass spectrum – the sputtered atoms and their ionisation probabilities yield a mass spectrum with a natural isotope pattern. A low work function markedly increases the yield of negative secondary ions.

Particle trajectories and parameter studies

In addition to the integral key figures, individual collision cascades can be followed and entire parameter ranges computed automatically.

Verification and comparison

The models are checked against established reference values, analytical model curves and physical consistency conditions.

  • Reference case argon on tungsten – at 1000 eV and normal incidence, the particle reflection is 0.34, the energy reflection 0.10 and the sputter yield 1.05. These values agree with the established RustBCA benchmarks, which in turn have been validated against SDTrimSP and experiment.
  • Mass ratio and backscattering – the pronounced difference between heavy and light targets is reproduced correctly. Argon is strongly reflected from tungsten, whereas on titanium, of almost the same mass, argon mostly penetrates and sputters more strongly.
  • Analytical model curves – the Thompson distribution for the sputter spectrum and yield and reflection formulae of the Bohdansky–Yamamura and Eckstein type are fitted to the simulated points. The derived parameters and the coefficient of determination are reported.
  • Consistency for compounds – the reflection of an alloy reproduces that of its main component; brass behaves like copper, stainless steel like iron.
  • Charge model – the computed survival fraction of argon on metals stays below one per cent over the entire energy range, in line with experience from low-energy ion scattering spectroscopy. For oxygen, the negative channel yields high fractions as expected; for nitrogen, almost none.

Range of validity

The following limitations arise from the underlying models and should be taken into account when interpreting results.

  • Lower energy limit – the binary collision approximation assumes that a particle essentially collides with one target atom at a time. Below about 10 to 20 eV, many-body and binding effects become important; results in this range should be read as trends.
  • Calibration of the charge model – the characteristic velocities of the charge-exchange model are system-dependent. Without calibration against measured data, the model provides reliable trends; for quantitative absolute values, an adjustment is required. The program includes a calibration function for this purpose.
  • Reactive species – for oxygen, nitrogen and molecular ions, the collision kinematics are as reliable as for noble gases, but charge exchange is only qualitative, since it proceeds via different mechanisms.
  • Static target – the material is treated as homogeneous and amorphous. Fluence-dependent effects such as roughening, implantation or changes in composition during operation are not tracked.
  • Curved and rough geometries – these modes use a different geometry kernel and are suitable for comparing rough and smooth surfaces within the same mode, not as a substitute for the validated computation on the flat surface.
  • Atomic secondary particles – only single atoms are sputtered. Molecular and cluster ions, as they occur in real secondary ion mass spectra, are not modelled.

Download

The software is provided as an installer for Windows and includes all required components, including the simulation kernel. A separate installation of Python or other libraries is not required. By default, the software is installed for the current user account only, requires no administrator rights and does not change any system-wide settings.

What's new in version 1.0.0

First publicly released version. It comprises the complete functionality developed so far:

  • Reflection and sputtering with energy, angular and depth distributions, and energy–angle correlation.
  • Charge exchange as a three-channel model with positive, neutral and negative fractions, including a calibration function.
  • Secondary ions and the derived SIMS mass spectrum with natural isotope distribution.
  • Compounds and alloys with element-resolved analysis of preferential sputtering.
  • Five surface geometries, from the flat surface to an imported triangle mesh.
  • Selectable interaction potentials, numerical methods and surface binding models.
  • Automated parameter studies over energy, angle of incidence and material selection with progressive display.
  • Graphics export with selectable background, language, resolution and colour palette, and data export as CSV with a complete metadata header.
  • Built-in guide with the physical background of all input quantities.

Note on Windows installation. The installer is currently not digitally signed. Windows may therefore display a warning about an unknown or unrecognised app when it is started. The installation can be continued via “More info” and then “Run anyway”. The SHA-256 checksum given above allows the downloaded file to be verified beforehand.

Note on where results are stored. Simulation results are not stored in the installation directory, but in the user profile under %LOCALAPPDATA%\IonSurfaceInteraction\results\. Uninstalling via “Apps & features” removes the program; results already generated are retained and can be deleted manually if required.

IONTRACE

IONTRACE is a simulation code for static and time-dependent ion optics, in particular for the extraction of ions from gridded ion sources. Computations are either fully three-dimensional or translationally symmetric in two dimensions. Electric field, ion trajectories and space charge are computed self-consistently. In addition, neutral gas, charge exchange, electrode erosion and the resulting grid lifetime can be investigated.

What is IONTRACE for?

The extraction optics largely determine the beam current, the beam shape and the lifetime of the grids. These quantities are closely coupled: space charge affects the electric field and hence the ion trajectories. Charge-exchange collisions between beam ions and the neutral gas create slow ions, which are accelerated by the electric field towards the grids, where they can cause erosion. The progressive change in grid geometry in turn affects the electric field and the extracted ion beam.

IONTRACE captures these interactions in a coupled three-dimensional simulation. The grid geometry can be updated over successive operating intervals, so that the effects of erosion on the field, particle trajectories and neutral gas distribution can be taken into account.

Physical models and features

The functionality is modular. Depending on the task, the following models and analyses can be combined.

  • Two- and three-dimensional computation – the same software computes either fully in three dimensions or translationally symmetric in two dimensions. The 2D mode uses the same interface and the same analyses, but solves large grids much faster, which makes it suitable for preliminary studies and for translationally symmetric arrangements such as the cylindrical analyser.
  • Plasma and ion emission – models for self-consistent plasma extraction with Boltzmann electrons, and for prescribed emitter surfaces and initial distributions.
  • Electric field computation – solution of the Poisson or Poisson–Boltzmann equation including space charge. Multigrid, SOR and direct solvers are available for the resulting systems of linear equations.
  • Coupled particle computation – ion trajectories and space-charge density are coupled iteratively with the electric field.
  • Time-dependent potentials – time-varying electrode voltages allow dynamic ion optics to be studied, for example time-of-flight arrangements.
  • Curved electrode surfaces – either the classic staircase representation or the Shortley–Weller sub-cell correction for more accurate representation of curved boundaries.
  • Symmetries and multiple apertures – mirror boundary conditions allow reduced computational domains. Geometries with multiple apertures can also be studied in a common computational domain.
  • Geometry creation – parametric construction from geometric primitives – including cylinder segment, half-cylinder and curved electrodes – that can be positioned freely in space and rotated by arbitrary angles, and a readable, text-based, version-controllable file format.
  • Neutral gas – test-particle Monte Carlo simulation of the spatial neutral gas distribution within the electrode geometry.
  • Charge exchange – generation and tracking of charge-exchange ions based on the local neutral gas density, the ion beam and the corresponding cross-sections. Locations of origin and electrode impact points can be analysed spatially.
  • Erosion and lifetime – computation of material-, energy- and angle-dependent electrode erosion. The progressively changing geometry can be carried over into the subsequent field, particle and neutral gas computations.
  • Visualisation and analysis – integrated 3D display, convergence and progress monitoring, and export of simulation and analysis data.

Fields and trajectories

All figures come from the same simulation run of a two-grid optics system with a screen grid at +1400 V and an accelerator grid at −180 V.

Neutral gas and charge exchange

Where charge-exchange ions are created depends on the spatial overlap of the ion beam with the neutral gas. The electric field then determines their trajectories and impact energies. Together with the material properties, this yields the spatial distribution of electrode erosion.

Erosion and lifetime

The local erosion rate is computed from the spatial impact rate of the ions, their energies and angles of incidence, and the material-dependent sputter yield. By updating the grid geometry over time, its evolution over the operating period can be studied and the lifetime estimated.

Verification and comparison

The individual models are checked against analytical reference cases, mesh convergence studies and comparisons with independent simulation programs.

  • Analytical verification – comparison of the electrostatic field solver with the exact solution for a parallel-plate capacitor.
  • Mesh convergence – study of the solution on increasingly finer meshes and comparison of the classic staircase representation with the Shortley–Weller sub-cell treatment.
  • Comparison with KOBRA – comparison of selected field, beam and current quantities for a common reference case. Remaining differences are assessed taking into account the respective plasma and emission models.
  • Testing the erosion computation – defined test cases for the processing of ion impacts, the computation of material removal and the step-by-step update of the geometry.
  • Verification of the neutral gas computation – comparison of the computed transmission through a cylindrical aperture with the corresponding Clausing factor in the molecular flow regime.

Manual

The user manual describes plasma ion extraction step by step using the supplied example setup: from the geometry via plasma, solver and convergence to residual gas, charge exchange, erosion, lifetime computation and parameter studies. Numerous screenshots show the interface and the analyses; a dedicated chapter covers how to check the reliability of a result. The manual is freely available and can be read in the browser without installation.

What's new in version 2.1.2

Version 2.1.2 replaces 2.1.1 and fixes an error in the supplied example setups. All new features from 2.1.1 are included.

  • Charge exchange in the REX-3G examples. The four example setups now explicitly specify xenon as the beam species and as the collision partner for charge exchange. Previously only the ion mass was given in the setup; anyone who enabled charge exchange had it rejected. The message for a missing species now names the fields Ion species and Collision partner, in which the species is to be selected.

What's new in version 2.1.1

Version 2.1.1 completes the English interface, takes the ion temperature into account when launching particles, and brings the display of the eroded wall into line with the computation. For the first time there is a user manual. Thermal spread of the ions and the new wall position for charge-exchange ions are enabled by default; numerical results may therefore differ from 2.1.0. Setups and results from 2.1.0 should still open, without guarantee. As before, the connection to computing clusters is not part of the installation package.

  • User manual. An English-language manual describes plasma ion extraction from geometry to lifetime computation using the supplied example setup, with reference values to reproduce and a chapter on checking the reliability of a result. It is available on this page as a PDF.
  • English interface completed. The labels were systematically checked for remaining German text and completed, among others in the status lines of the convergence tab, the key-figure cards, the descriptions of the sputter models, the parameter study, and the export and body dialogs. Controls that did not respond in English were fixed along the way: the sweep button of the beam line, the point-cloud selection in the beam profile and the preview button of the setup wizard.
  • Ion temperature in particle launch. The ion temperature spreads the initial velocity of the ions transverse to the beam direction and longitudinally around the Bohm velocity; previously all ions started exactly parallel. The spread can be switched in the plasma dialog; when switched off, the program computes as before.
  • Estimating the fraction of doubly charged xenon ions. A suggestion button next to the input field estimates the fraction from the electron temperature, plasma density and neutral gas density of the discharge: direct double ionisation after Rejoub et al. (2002), stepwise ionisation via Xe+ with the measured cross-section of Bell, Djurić and Dunn (1993). Only values that are explicitly accepted are entered.
  • Wall position for charge-exchange ions. Whether a charge-exchange ion hits the wall is now checked against the same wall that the field solver uses. Previously, for these ions it was offset by half a cell width; in computational cells with two quarter apertures, one aperture therefore acted too wide and the other too narrow.
  • Eroded wall in display and analysis. The field tabs draw the eroded contour from the fill level of the cells, i.e. the wall actually used in the computation; thin material remnants no longer appear as teeth. For a snapshot of the lifetime run, the 3D view shows its wall and the impacts of the corresponding erosion round. The radius profile of the bore extends over the entire length of the electrode, including the end layers.

What's new in version 2.1.0

Version 2.1.0 extends the beam and charge exchange to mixed charge states and custom cross-section tables, considerably reduces the memory requirements of long lifetime runs and speeds up the analysis tabs. Setups and results from 2.0.1 should still open, without guarantee. As before, the connection to computing clusters is not part of the installation package.

  • Mixed charge states in the beam. The beam can contain singly and doubly charged ions at the same time; the fraction is specified as a particle or current fraction and enters the field computation via space charge and plasma density. Directly impinging ions contribute to erosion with their own energy and particle current for each charge state. Charge exchange for Xe2+ takes into account double and single exchange after Miller (2002), and the lifetime run includes the mixture.
  • Custom charge-exchange cross-sections. Cross-sections can be loaded as a custom table. The file header names the reaction as well as the reference frame and unit of energy; a help button explains the format. Custom values are used as soon as the setup names their reaction.
  • Erosion at the aperture wall. By default, the angle of incidence now follows the already eroded wall instead of the initial shape, and the aperture wall keeps its direction up to both ends of the plate. For computational cells with several apertures, the eroded volume is reported per aperture.
  • Lower memory requirements. A lifetime run now keeps only one trajectory set in memory, and the charge-exchange computation no longer needs a stored trajectory set. Before the start, the memory requirement is estimated and the user is asked if memory is tight; during the run, the live window shows memory usage.
  • Log of the lifetime run. Each run automatically writes a file to its results folder: setup, operating point and model choices in the header, one line per erosion step and a summary with termination reason, totals and run time. Even an aborted run is thus fully documented.
  • Faster analysis. The energy tab evaluates all trajectories in a single pass, and the field sections redraw only once after a rebuild. The upper limit on displayed trajectories now also applies to the field maps, and switching tabs no longer redraws a finished image.
  • Setups and comparison computations. The operational computation of the residual gas now also handles mirror cells with two quarter channels; the corresponding hex cells are included as setups. For krypton, a comparison of the yield computation with the measurements of Manova et al. (2026) is documented, including the deviations of the model.
  • Operation and export. A loaded setup retains its residual gas map and its lifetime settings. Settings windows open at the size their content requires, the 3D view of the setup tab shows the particle launch surface and appears correctly in the saved image, and the erosion export writes the outline of the entire electrodes including the boundary of the computational domain.

What's new in version 2.0.1

The step to version 2 brings together three developments: the software has become bilingual, the models for ion species and material removal rest on a documented and referenced basis, and the lifetime computation takes into account how erosion itself feeds back on residual gas and field. Setups and results from 1.2.1 should still open, without guarantee. The terms under which the software may be used are shown in the interface at start-up and must be confirmed there. As before, the connection to computing clusters is not part of the installation package.

  • Bilingual operation. The entire interface can be switched between German and English: menus, settings, explanations, messages, tables, the axis and legend texts of all plots, and the wizard for new setups. English is the default. The installer also follows the system language.
  • Reorganised interface. The start area offers the three ways into a computation directly and leads from there to the supplied example setups. The controls of a results tab are arranged in a column next to the image, the most important key figures as cards. Expert values can be hidden behind a switch, and numeric fields take effect only once the input is confirmed.
  • Ion species and charge exchange. An ion species is defined by its composition; mass and charge state follow from it and are two separate quantities. In addition to xenon and krypton, nitrogen is included with its own charge-exchange cross-section, with a source reference. Molecular ions fly as a whole particle and are split into their atoms only at the surface. Every energy value states which energy it refers to.
  • Models for material removal. The yield computation after Yamamura and Tawara is implemented faithfully to the source, NIFS-DATA-23, including Thomas–Fermi stopping, the electronic denominator and the published material parameters. As a second model, the description by Tartz and Neumann (2007) for xenon on carbon is available, plus a mixed option that selects the appropriate model for each material. The yield can be adjusted to your own measurements with a factor; the comparison with published measurements is documented, including where the model deviates.
  • Lifetime and erosion with feedback. The residual gas map is computed on the actual, widened channel shape, and each erosion step starts from the field of the previous one. The neutral gas transparency is recorded over the run time, and a completed run can be extended by further hours without starting from scratch. Control images are produced for every time step; also new are a model-independent impact list per erosion step, which can later be re-evaluated with other assumptions, and a balance of the redeposited material.
  • Shorter computation times. The field solver runs in a separate process, and the program remains responsive during a run. The solver tolerance adapts as convergence progresses, the charge-exchange pass uses all cores, and the residual gas run takes a tenth of the previous time. Parameter studies compute several points simultaneously, and program start-up takes only half as long.
  • Analysis and image output. A measuring cursor reads values in the section and surface images. Every results tab states from which computation step its image originates, and outputs its numbers as a table at the push of a button. A dedicated export window for publications allows freely placed labels and outputs both raster and vector formats; field lines and equipotential lines have been added as display options.
  • Example setups and comparison computations. Included is a freely designed three-grid stack in four variants, with which the software can be tried out without your own geometry data. For validation, setups based on published work have been added, including a hexagonal unit cell for xenon and krypton and a comparison with a 2500-hour endurance test.

What's new in version 1.2.1

The computational core of the three-dimensional simulation has remained unchanged; existing setups and results from 1.1.1 can still be opened. As before, the HPC connection is not part of the installation package.

  • Two-dimensional computation mode. IONTRACE now computes either three-dimensionally or translationally symmetric in two dimensions. The 2D mode is not a second piece of software, but a computation mode within the same program: same interface, same open dialog, same tabs. Large grids solve much faster, the computation can be aborted while running, and the computational mesh can be displayed. A 2D model of the 127-degree cylindrical analyser is included as a complete application example.
  • Residual gas from the operating data of the source. The neutral gas state is determined from current, gas flow and efficiency instead of from a density set by hand. The operational computation runs over three pages with a readiness check, a sketch of the real geometry and an accuracy estimate. The map shows the local density from the velocity moments instead of an average.
  • Freely rotatable bodies in the geometry. Bodies can be positioned freely in space and rotated by arbitrary angles; the body shape remains true to scale. New are cylinder segment, half-cylinder and curved electrodes. Composite bodies may be assigned their own potential, and the section plane can be rotated by 90 degrees.
  • More accurate erosion and lifetime computation. The aperture radius is determined from the open area instead of from a threshold; this eliminates a systematic offset of almost three micrometres. In addition to the ring average, the erosion at the deepest point is now shown – the ring average alone considerably dilutes local pitting. The eroded area is displayed between the initial and final contours, and the erosion history can be written as a video.
  • Parameter studies and time-of-flight path. A free voltage without an assigned formula can also be swept as a study variable; individual parameter points can be viewed specifically, and failed variants state a named reason instead of a number. The tabs of the time-of-flight path are once again linked to the time-of-flight result, and the lines of the time-of-flight spectrum are additionally listed in a table.
  • Revised image export. Title, legend and colour scale can be placed in the export image with the mouse. Field images drawn to scale are centred instead of at the right edge, field lines and arrows follow the selected colour palette, and the canvas is no longer inflated.
  • Continuous diagnostics. No program exit goes without a diagnostic entry: each session writes its own folder with a heartbeat and a closing message. An external watchdog detects hanging sessions, but never restarts or terminates anything on its own. A stress test with 472 individual checks was passed.

What's new in version 1.1.1

  • New export dialog for all figures. Every graphic is saved via the same dialog: on the left a preview of what is actually written to the file, on the right the settings. Size and font are specified in centimetres and typographic points and therefore no longer depend on window size, screen resolution or display scaling. The entered dimensions are binding: 16 × 11 cm results in a file measuring 16 × 11 cm.
  • Faulty SVG and EPS files fixed. In SVG, the labels now remain selectable text instead of mere outlines; EPS receives the required end-of-file marker and a verified bounding box. Transparency that EPS cannot represent is either blended with the background or rasterised — instead of silently being drawn opaque. Overlong axis labels are no longer cut off in any format.
  • Export profiles and defaults. Settings are remembered per file format; custom profiles can be created, renamed, imported and exported, and set as the default for a format or a plot type. Profiles for single-column and double-column publications and for presentations are included.
  • Selectable image content. Title, legend, electrode and potential labels and hint texts can be shown or hidden individually for export without changing the display in the program.
  • Revised results area for parameter studies. The space now belongs to the plot: a narrow tool column with the toggles Curves, Display and Analysis, a two-line header with study selection and status, readable curve names and an export menu.
  • Progress monitor for parameter studies. During a run, the characteristic curve builds up live: electrode currents versus the varied parameter, together with a variant table with status, computation time and error messages, and an estimate of the remaining time.
  • Truncated labels in the sidebars fixed; their width now adapts to the content and grows with the font size and the Windows display scaling.

What's new in version 1.1.0

  • Considerably faster field computation thanks to a parallelised AMG solver.
  • Optimised memory management and faster processing of particle trajectories.
  • Improved runtime diagnostics and numerous stability and detail improvements.
